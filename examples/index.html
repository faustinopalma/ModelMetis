Proof of concept / acoustic condition recognition

ModelMetis recognizes machine condition from sound

ModelMetis records a machine, turns the audio into signal-processing diagrams and measurements, and lets a general multimodal model compare them with reference recordings of known conditions. It names the matching condition and cites the evidence, or sends the case to a person.

The proof of concept works on public motor datasets recorded at changing speeds, loads and microphone positions. A production test bench runs every unit at set-points chosen in advance, so its recordings are directly comparable from the start.

How the method works

Each decision follows five steps. Every step produces a file a person can open, so each answer can be traced back to the sound.

  1. Record at a known regime

    The machine runs at a set speed and load while a fixed microphone records ten seconds of sound.

  2. Normalize to the shaft

    When speeds differ, the audio is resampled to its own shaft rotation, so rotation-locked tones sit at the same order in every recording.

  3. Analyze

    A deterministic signal-processing report shows spectra, spectrogram, envelope, cycle average and their measurements, in hertz and in shaft orders.

  4. Compare

    A general multimodal model studies the unknown report beside one reference report per known condition and cites figures and numbers from both.

  5. Decide

    It names the best-supported condition, or answers different so that a person reviews the case.

The model receives evidence it can read: diagrams and measurements of known physical meaning. A new condition joins the system as one more reference recording, and a new machine type as one more reference set.

Built for controlled test benches

The method is strongest when the reference and the unit under test are recorded in the same conditions. Industrial benches provide exactly that: automated set-points, fixed microphones and a known operating sequence. Two deployments follow directly.

End-of-line quality assessment

  1. The motor arrives at an automated bench and is driven through regimes fixed in advance.
  2. Microphones in fixed positions record the noise at each regime.
  3. Each recording is compared with references of good units and of known defects recorded at the same regime.
  4. The bench returns pass, a named defect with its evidence, or review.

Vehicle diagnostics in service

  1. The vehicle arrives at the workshop and is placed in standard conditions, such as idle and set engine speeds.
  2. A fixed microphone setup records each condition.
  3. Recordings are compared with references of the same model in the same conditions.
  4. The technician receives the recognized condition with the diagrams behind it, or a case flagged for inspection.

A pilot needs one reference recording per condition and regime, the speed set-point or a tachometer signal, a fixed microphone position and a review step for different answers. Without a speed signal, the base frequency can be estimated from the audio: on MAFAULDA the estimate agreed with the tachometer within 3% for 797 of 798 accepted recordings.

Next step: a pilot on a real bench

The proof of concept used three public datasets with publisher labels and few machines per condition. A pilot records references at the bench's own set-points, runs a batch of units through the same sequence and compares the decisions with the existing quality outcome. Because each recording is reproducible at its regime, the pilot measures the method in the conditions it was built for.
